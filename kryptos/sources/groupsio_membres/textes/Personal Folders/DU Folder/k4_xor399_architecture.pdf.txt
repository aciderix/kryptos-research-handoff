Throwing bits at K4:
Evidence for a Three-Zone Split with Tableau-Keyed
XOR Core
DRAFT
D. Umanovskis∗
June 2026 — draft
Abstract
This paper presents a theory that K4, the unsolved 97-letter passage of Kryptos, is split
into three zones, enciphered with different, most likely two, methods. A 61-letter core body
(positions 25–85) is enciphered by a five-bit XOR against the sculpture’s own engraved
Vigen`ere tableau, read from the start of row M and aligned so that the row’s first letter
falls immediately after the opening four-letter group OBKR, whose first two letters. The head
(positions 0–24) and tail (86–96) flanks are enciphered by a substitution method, with no
XOR or use of the tableau. We present the statistical evidence for these conclusions and
show an example of how the known BERLINCLOCK crib would have been encrypted. The
paper makes no claim of recovering additional plaintext of K4 beyond what is publicly
known.

Methodology
Table 1 defines the terms we use in this paper.
Of particular importance to our method was the shuffle null, informally answering the ques-
tion “would this finding be impressive if the ciphertext were meaningless?”. The search space for
algorithmic approaches is large enough that algorithms will inevitably produce tempting looking
partial matches. Examples the authors have seen include MSGFORCIA and BERLINCLOCKSPOOKSHAD.
The infamous “K4 syndrome” is a tendency for an attempted solver of K4 to develop increas-
ingly convoluted methods that go against the evidence and certainly seeing seemingly legible
decryptions is a risk for going down that path. As such, we used shuffle nulls to protect our-
selves during all algorithmic runs. We take the K4 ciphertext, scramble its letters into a random
order, destroying any positional structure, and run the analysis on the scrambled copy. Doing
this with a large number of scrambled texts lets us properly judge the significance of search
results obtained when run against the real K4.
We index ciphertext positions as 0-based, 0–96. Panel positions index the 867-character
engraved tableau.

In accordance with Sanborn’s statements, we rule out any method that requires knowledge
of the K1-K3 plaintext. As such we don’t consider using the coordinates from K2 or any other
part of the first three messages.
We also preemptively rule out any methods that are based on the particulars of the World
Clock in Berlin. The first crib release happened twenty years after the sculpture was completed,
it stands to reason that the message was intended to be decrypted without knowing it refers
to the clock.
We similarly rule out any method requiring external information, such as book ciphers, or
access to the actual sculpture. Our work assumes that an accurate transcript of the sculpture
is enough to solve K4.
2.2
Initial assumptions
Our investigation was, before arriving at the results, guided by a number of clues and assump-
tions. These all appear reasonable to us but cannot be proven definitvely. We list the clues
and our assumptions below.
• Ed Scheidt asked “How do you deliver a message in such a way that it conveys not only
the encrypted data, but also the key?”. Jim Sanborn has stated “I used a bit of stego
[steganography] when designing the fourth part of Kryptos.”.
• The sculpture has a much-discussed irregularity in the engraving of the K3 ciphertext.
A five-letter block DYAHR appears visually distinct, with the letters Y, A, R in it ele-
vated above the baseline. We assume this is significant and a strong candidate for a
steganographic hint in the sculpture.
• Sanborn has multiple time referenced that he was taught “modern” encryption systems.
We consider it likely he would have been taught at least the basics of digital encryption,
3

[[page 4]]
such as the principles behind DES. As bitwise XOR is a fundamental operation in many
digital encryption systems, and is easily doable (and teachable) on paper, we consider it
near certain that Sanborn was familiar with XOR.
Overall, our starting hypothesis was that, while K1-K3 are purely classical ciphers, K4 is a
classical/digital hybrid, with a classical layer and a digital layer. The digital layer is assumed to
be a bitwise operation, and it is assumed that the sculpture itself provides some steganographic
hint about K4.
2.3
H¨orenberg’s work
Cryptanalyst Michael H¨orenberg has previously made observations in [1] that inform our digital
approach to K4. H¨orenberg assumed that the DYAHR anomaly was to be interpreted as a five-bit
pattern, an assumption we do not rely on a priori but that our work corroborates. H¨orenberg
also used the tableau as a key stream source, which our work also does.
Several assumptions put forward in [1] have been definitely shown to be incorrect, as they
contradict later information about K4, but our method independently arrives at the same
running key as this earlier work.
3
Hiding in plain sight
3.1
The tableau as a key stream
If XOR encryption was used for a 97-letter text, a key of at least that length is needed.
Examining the sculpture provides a candidate for the source of a key stream - the tableau
panel. The sculpture’s second panel carries a Vigen`ere tableau keyed by KRYPTOS. Figure 2
reproduces the whole side as engraved. Read top left to bottom right, it is a single linear
stream of letters: a 30-character header row (ABCD...XYZABCD), then 26 lettered rows of 31
characters each (row-label plus 30 content characters), and the header row is repeated again at
the bottom. An unexplained quirk is that the N row has an extra character, a second L, being
32 characters long. In total,
30 + 26 × 31 + 1 + 30 = 867 letters.
A K4 candidate key is a 97-letter run of this stream, so candidates are determined by their
starting position d on the panel. Disregarding the possibility of wrapped keys, the key readout
has to fit inside the stream so d can be 0 through 867 −97 = 770, giving 771 candidate keys.
Alternatively, the key could be taken from the ciphertext panel as well, so a 97-long stretch
of ciphertext preceding K4. This did not seem intuitive to us but we did run the ciphertext-
as-key scan as control as well, as will be shown later.
3.2
The key search
Under this hypothesis, the first experiment is to evaluate each of the 771 possible tableau keys.
For every offset d, take the 97-long key running from d to d + 97, XOR the K4 ciphertext with
that key and measure the IoC to see if a non-random structure appears.
To perform the XOR, there are two questions that must be answered first.
1. What numerical values to assign to letters?
2. What rule to use for positions where the result of XOR is not a letter?
For numerical representation of letters, we tried several intuitive encodings.

After applying the XOR to cipher and key, Ci ⊕Ki, if the result r was outside the range
that letters are represented by, we tried several rules.
• Passthrough. Output the ciphertext letter Ci, unmodified.
• Output the keystream letter Ki.
• Modulo 26, so 27 becomes A, 28 becomes B, etc. 0 →Z.
• Truncate to the four lowest bits. Values 27 — 31 become K, L, M, N, O. 0 →Z.
• Reflect the result, so 52 −r. 27 is same as 25 (Y), 28 is as 24 (X). 0 →Z.
Some of those choices like the inclusion of Baudot encoding or the “reflect” style rule
appeared unlikely but the computational cost of these scans is effectively zero so we erred on
the side of checking more.

Key alignment
The first experiment, summarised in Table 2, showed an interesting pattern exists somewhere

prominent peak, the gap between it and the second spot is bigger than the gap between #2

3.4
K1-K3 as a placebo
Our control was, as mentioned, synthetic texts obtained by randomly shuffling the letters of
K4. This control may miss artefacts present in real ciphers so as an additional control, we ran
the same key scan on 97-character blocks of K2 and K3, as well as the entire K1. We also
ran the algorithm against K1-K3 plaintexts as a sample of real English. None of these runs
produced anything of statistical significance, leaving us with a result that doesn’t replicate
outside of K4.

Body and flanks
4.1
The localisation scan
After making the observations detailed in the previous section, the authors spent some effort
to extract additional plaintext or structural information from K4 but were unable to make any
progress worth reporting.
Progress resumed after thinking through the IoC of 0.0573 that the message has after
applying the d = 399 XOR key. This statistically significant IoC is well above random but
also well below English and the intermediate stream, after reverting XOR, resisted the authors’
attempts.
Then we hypothesised that the XOR key may fit only part of K4, with the rest of the
message using no XOR. The IoC of 0.0573 would then likely be a lower-than-actual value,
diluted by the part where the XOR should not be applied.
To test this, we generalised the scan (Algorithm box 2): over every combination of offset
d and contiguous window [a, a + ℓ) with ℓ≥20, score the window’s IoC as a z-value against
shuffle bands matched to that window length. This is 771 × 3081 = 2,375,451 geometries.

Visualizing the key like that also highlights properties that intuitively seem noteworthy and

5
Pointing at M
The candidate key alignment starts its readout from panel position 399. Row M, the important
one, starts its content at panel position 403 and the XOR begins to be applied starting with
ABC... from row M, so position 424. Thus NORTHEAST is enciphered with ABCDEFGHI.
There are two features of Kryptos that we consider as possible pointers to row M, and the
decryption method.
First is the interpretation of the three raised letters in DYAHR. If this is read as a five-bit
sequence with the raised letters being 1, the result is 011012 in binary or 13, which is the letter
M. This observation was made by H¨orenberg in [1], where it’s treated as pure five-bit ASCII.
We note that a binary representation of letters in five-bit ASCII is equivalent to a decimal
representation of alphabet-indexed letters. The DYAHR hint can thus be seen as saying both
“use bitwise techniques” and “row M is significant”.
The second hint may be the start of K4’s own ciphertext. The first two letters are OB, and
11

[[page 12]]
performing an XOR on their values also points to M.
O ⊕B = 15 ⊕2 = 13 = ‘M’
We speculate that the letters OB are such an intentional hint.
This would make OB an
indicator, a common technique (used by Enigma among others) where part of the transmitted
cipher, usually at the beginning, is information about the key.
Making OB an indicator would be very challenging if the XOR applied to the entire K4
ciphertext. If however the XOR only applies to the body zone, as we propose, it would be
much easier to encrypt the flanks in a way that the first two letters are OB, indeed it would be
trivial if the flanks use a substitution of some kind.
6
Narrowing the zones
In earlier sections we located a tableau key and a body window to which it applies by search.
In this section we look at some alternative hypotheses and we also examine how precisely we
know the boundaries of the body and flank zones.
If the first two letters OB are meant to be an indicator for the XOR key, there is an obviously
simpler cipher design. Leave the indicator letters exactly as they are in the plaintext and then
apply masking and XOR to the rest of the message. So the plaintext begins with OB (there are
certainly many English words starting so) and the remaining 95 characters are all masked by
the unknown procedure and then XORed with the key.
We cannot definitively rule out such a construction method, but the statistics given in 4.2
are against it. If K4 is indeed split into a body and flanks, we want to narrow down the exact
boundaries.
6.1
Head–body boundary
Our localisation scan cannot confidently indicate the zone boundaries. Moving a window by
one position back, while keeping the length, will at worst exchange one correct position for
noise, barely affecting the IoC. The per-length winners in Table 5 have a boundary at 25 or
26. So the scan leaves us with a ±1 resolution.
No per-length winner in the scans started at 24 or earlier. Given the choice between position
25 or 26 as the body start, we pick 25 mainly due to a plaintext boundary, with some support
from Bayesian modeling.
Our global offset scan and the localisation scan operated on the K4 ciphertext and the
sculpture’s tableau, without any knowledge of the cribs.
The known plaintext EAST is at
positions 21–24 and NORTHEAST starts at 25. If the encryption method changes at the boundary,
it seems far more natural for the boundary to be at position 25, splitting EAST|NORTHEAST in
a way that aligns with the English words of the plaintext.
We also ran an ABC (Approximate Bayesian Computation) model generating 3 million
synthetic K4s (using the known cribs, the remaining characters filled with English words) and
applying a XOR key from a random panel offset to a random window. The windows were given
start locations at positions 21 −33 and a length of 50 −70. We scored these synthetic K4s
on sixteen dimensions, of which eight contain no IoC measurement (five top letter frequencies,
XOR passthrough fraction, autocorrelation, flank-body TVD) and eight were different IoC
computations 1.
Comparing the synthetic K4s against the real one, the ABC posterior scored position 25
as the most likely, in the full 16-dimension evaluation and the 8-dimension IoC-free evaluation.

7
Decryption possibilities
Under the proposed K4 architecture, we have three zones with different encryption methods.
The individual flanks, head and tail, are short and we assume that both flanks use the same
encryption method, which makes K4 a two-cipher message.
In this section we examine what the three zone split and the XOR key tell us about the
possibilities of retrieving plaintext. The authors have not given this question an exhaustive
examination but the initial findings do not show any clear path towards decrypting K4 assuming
the paper’s architecture.
7.1
Body’s stream cipher
After undoing the XOR operation on the body, we get the intermediate stream, which is itself
the result of an unknown masking cipher and contains two cribs.
Unfortunately, the obtained stream does not offer many pointers. It has many S characters,
indicating that the masking cipher merges letters, and the known cribs do not fit any simple
substitution model. We see that four different plain text letters (E, O, R, S) become S in the
stream, with other many-to-one mappings also existing.

The observed patterns do allow for two substitution methods at the masking layer that the
authors identified.
There could be two substitution tables, call them T0 and T1, applied in a period-7 pattern
(T0, T1, T0, T0, T0, T1, T1). Each table maps one or two plaintext letters to a stream letter.
Under this two-table reading, the plaintext appears unrecoverable. This is also just one fit
among several, the cribs are also consistent with a longer period or more tables.
The same observations could be explained by an occurrence counting system, where each
plaintext letter has a list of (likely two) stream letters, with the first occurrence of the plaintext
letter being substituted for the first letter on the list, etc. Under this scheme there is likewise
insufficient information to recover the plaintext.
Regardless of the exact scheme, the problem is evident in the NORTHEAST crib alone. The
stream contains three letters S that map to different plaintext letters, which is a strong alphabet
collapse. Such an encryption scheme is possible but it is likely not reversible without knowledge
of the exact process.
A particularly interesting segment is at the end of the body stream. We see SSZSFSSPFT
ending the stream, a segment very heavy on the letter S and significantly contributing to the
measured IoC. In the S-heavy body, 12 of 14 occurrences are from XOR, not passthrough, and
the XOR of two uniformly-distributed letters favors small output values so we note this as an
interesting pattern.
We emphasise that this is not a definitive conclusion as more investigation of the stream as
ciphertext can be conducted.
7.2
The flank cipher
For our investigation of the flanks, we treated them both as a continuous span of ciphertext
encrypted by one unknown method. The flanks contain one crib, EAST.
The authors hit a dead end quite rapidly with the flanks. A monoalphabetic substitution
cipher is possible but places difficult constraints on the plaintext (T may not appear outside
14

[[page 15]]
of EAST, only one more appearance of E) and we were not able to obtain even speculatively
meaningful tilings. A polyalphabetic substitution for the flanks is minimally constraining and
admits almost any text.
A Vigen´ere search similar to K1-K2 turned up nothing for the flanks, and a brute force
search of ACA Gromark in the standard and KRYPTOS-keyed alphabets likewise produced
no results. Some other classical ciphers are excluded due to the flank structure, like Playfair
being impossible due to the BB digraph.
8
Conclusion
We have proposed a three-zone structure for K4, where the middle zone is to be decrypted by
XORing it with a running key from the tableau panel.
The flanks, the head and tail zones, use a different mechanism.
The architecture, if correct, would explain the meaning of the raised letters in DYAHR and
the reason for the extra L in one of the tableau’s rows.
At the present time, we do not see a clear path from the architecture to a plaintext recovery
and that remains an avenue for future work.
