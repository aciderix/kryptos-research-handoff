K3 ENCRYPTION MATRIX
While it may or may not be the actual matrix Jim Sanborn used, the simplest method I found for encrypting K3 was to pad
the plaintext with a seven-letter keyword (i.e., kryptos) and evenly distribute the text across an 86x4 grid.  The ciphertext
is readily obtained in reading every seventh column, upward, from right to left.

You’ll notice, however, there’s a discrepancy between the ciphertext generated by this matrix and that found on the
sculpture.  Specifically, the position of the question mark.  I suppose Jim could’ve transposed it post-encryption, but why?
If it were meant to make the cipher more difficult to crack, a character deeper in the text would’ve been a better choice.
(see Alternative Transposition Test)  Further, if “Q” is shorthand for “Question”, as I understood it, the question mark is
redundant.  Why end a sentence with double punctuation?
With a slight modification to the matrix above, the answer presents itself.

This matrix allows us to duplicate the ciphertext that appears on the sculpture, and may explain the offset characters
END|YAHR found on the sculpture as a clue for discovering the original matrix.  More importantly, it shows that the
question mark preceded the original plaintext message and may have been a continuation of K2.
