I've been thinking about the K4 cipher, and I wanted to share some of my observations. 
After some analysis, I think that JS chose the crib words "BERLIN" and "CLOCK" with the belief that they 
would be enough information to break the cipher. When that clue failed, he added "EASTNORTHEAST" to 
the mix.  
As a result, we have two sizable cribs, each solidly within their half of the cipher: 
 
 
----------------------EASTNORTHEAST-------------- 
---------------BERLINCLOCK----------------------- 
 
 
However, there isn't much overlap between these halves when folded like this, especially since "BERLIN" 
and "NORTHEAST" don't correspond to anything in the other half. 
But something interesting happens when you reverse one of the halves. Now the two cribs have 
significant positional overlaps! Maybe this is why "NORTHEAST" was the next clue after "BERLINCLOCK"? 
 
 
                      EASTNORTHEAST               
?OBKRUOXOGHULBSOLIFBBWFLRVQQPRNGKSSOTWTQSJQSSEKZZ 
                       KCOLCNILREB                
RACKEUAUHUKGIDCJTXZKDGWKPFZMTTVPYNBFNIWAIDULKJTAW 
 
 
Because the cipher has an odd length of 97 characters (i.e., ignoring the question mark), I don't think JS 
intended for the halves to be directly compared to one another.  Rather, I've been working on ideas that 
involve reversing either the plaintext or ciphertext (it's been easier to reverse the cipher so that the 
plaintext remains readable): 
 
 
----------------------EASTNORTHEAST----------------------------BERLINCLOCK-----------------------

important for K4 whereas the KRYPTOS alphabet alone was used for K1-K2)

To find the missing keytext, locate the plaintext letter across the top, follow that column down until you 
arrive at the ciphertext, and then follow that row to the left to get the keytext. 
B:F=S 
E:C=S 
R:J=K 
L:C=G 
I:L=N 
N: 
 
 
BERLINCLOCK (plaintext) 
SSKGNRPQQVR (ciphertext) 
FCJCLOBJGUR (keytext) 
 
 
What piqued my interest here is that CLOBJ portion... which is really close to CLOCK (if you shift the B 
and J by +1). And this is only offset by three characters from the plaintext, so maybe this is a form of 
autokey? The starting key could then be something like the YAR offset letters (which is RAY... reversed!).   
If this is a modified autokey, the next three characters after CLOCK are GUR... If you happen to shift each 
of these +1, you get HVS, which could be read as "HAS". 
 
 
FCJCLOBJGUR (autokey) 
66400011111 (shift) 
LINCLOCKHVS (corresponding plaintext) 
 
 
At this point, I couldn't derive a repeating pattern needed to shift the autokey for this to work 
universally. And, there seems to be nothing but noise on the EASTNORTHEAST side of the cipher when 
applying this method. So, I think I'm at a dead end here trying to make an autokey based on reversing 
text work. 
But, maybe somebody else will see a pattern that I can't?
