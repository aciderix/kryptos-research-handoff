A Change in Perspective 
 
Until now all previous solutions to the first 3 parts of Kryptos have been the result of “cracking” 
the code.  They may have been either pencil-and-paper solutions, or computer-aided, but they all 
relied on extracting the plain text from the cipher text through the use of clever techniques and 
plain old hard work. 
 
In late October 2003 something happened to change my perspective.  Elonka Dunin (co-
moderator of the Yahoo Kryptos Group) and I had an opportunity to spend some time with both 
Jim Sanborn (creator of Kryptos) and Ed Scheidt (former head of cypto for the CIA, as well as 
Sanborn’s advisor on Kryptos).     
 
Both were, as expected, very tight-lipped on the subject of Kryptos.  But, during conversations 
that spanned several days, a few things were mentioned, almost in passing, that changed the 
way I thought about Kryptos. 
 
Ed Scheidt talked about the differences between the older codes used in Kryptos and modern 
codes.  He focused on the problem of delivering the “key” to an operative in the field.  He pointed 
out that in modern “digital” codes the algorithm is known to everyone.  The “key” is simply a long 
number.  In older “analog” codes the “key is the algorithm.”  The question he posed:  “So how do

you deliver the key in a way that your operative can recover it, and still keep the secret from 
falling into the wrong hands?”  The implication was that the latter condition could be met through 
the use of intentional dead ends, but he provided no hints regarding the actual delivery of the 
algorithm. 
 
This prompted me to look at Kryptos from a slightly different perspective – not as simply an 
encrypted message, but also as a vehicle with which to deliver the key.  This changes the task 
from one of “cracking” a code to one much more like “solving” a puzzle. 
 
Jim Sanborn, talking to Elonka over dinner, pointed out that no one had solved Part 3 using the 
same matrix he had used to encrypt it. 
 
This was particularly interesting because the Kryptos Group had discovered multiple techniques 
for solving the transposition.  If a clue could be found in Kryptos that would point to one method 
as being the single correct solution, it could provide valuable information regarding the very 
nature of Kryptos; i.e., was it an encrypted message to be “cracked,” or a puzzle to be “solved?”   
As Part 3 also appeared to be the simplest section, it seemed to be the logical place to begin 
looking for hidden clues.

Recovering the Key Words for Parts 1 & 2 
 
 
The table is clearly a broad hint as to the method used to encrypt Parts 1 & 2 (with the ABCD… 
index across the top and side a possible dead end, as they are not used in the decryption 
process).  But, to put these sections at a level of difficulty (level of simplicity?) similar to that of 
Part 3, Kryptos must contain some mechanism for conveying the key words PALIMPSEST and 
ABSCISSA to the operative in the field.  
 
A likely place to hide those key words was in the table itself, possible by making a slight alteration 
to one letter in each line.  Unfortunately, due to resolution limitations and jpeg artifacts, the photos 
currently available are not sufficient to make a definitive determination.  However, they do provide 
some evidence that the serifs on letters corresponding to the key words have been shortened.  
Compare the P on line A (where the first letter of PALIMPSEST would be expected to be located) 
with the P on row B.  The portion of the serif extending to the left appears to be slightly shorter.

The frequency match is absolutely stunning. And the fact that one of the alphabets contained the 
word KRYPTOS provided added evidence that this was the correct substitution.  My speculation 
was that the few anomalies, the higher than expect number of Qs and Zs, could be a deliberate 
attempt to hide the frequency distribution from the casual observer – another dead end.  Any 
measurement of the IC (Index of Coincidence) would indicate that a simple mono-alphabetic 
substitution was not used, preventing anyone from even attempting to look for a match of 
frequency distributions. 
 
This Q & Z theory was give some credibility by a comment Scheidt made when asked if obscure 
languages were used in Kryptos.  As usual, he did not give a direct answer.  But the implication 
was clearly that foreign languages were considered, but ruled out.  Almost as an afterthought he 
added that it was possible to use text that “didn’t look like English.”

The question now becomes – is there any set of possible hidden clues that support the selection 
of these alphabets for the substitution? 
 
The GHI… alphabet immediately brought to mind Roger Anderson’s recent observation that the 
GHI line on the table contained an extra character at the end.2  Roger had already been doing 
some investigation regarding the significance of the alphabet on this line. 
 
Maybe that extra L is an indication to the operative that this is one of the alphabets to be used in 
the substitution.  However, my GHI… alphabet did not contain KRYPTOS.  Are we dealing with 
another misleading dead end?  Was the operative to use the L to find, not the alphabet, but as an 
indicator the alphabet should be shifted enough so that it starts with GHI…?   
 
This only made sense if there was some other set of instructions that would allow the operative to 
construct the ABC… alphabet as well, including the position of the letters KRYPTOS. 
 
Looking at that alphabet,  
 
ABCDEFGHIJLMNQUKRYPTOSVWXZ 
 
I noticed the QU and immediately thought of the “errors” in Parts 1 & 2.  I had always thought of 
these errors as the K & R errors, because those are the letters that show up in Kryptos.  
However, the errors in the plain text are Q and U.  Could these errors be the markers that locate 
KRYPTOS within the ABC… alphabet? 
 
While this does seem possible, unfortunately, that question can’t be answered for certain until a 
complete solution is found for Part 4. 
 
However, there is some additional (and quite circumstantial) evidence that the suggested 
substitution is indeed correct.  But, before I get to that, you may have noticed that my frequency 
distribution charts used only 90 of the 97 characters from Part 4.  That’s because, in the search 
for other possible clues, I noticed the KR TOS YP in the cipher text. 
 
?OBKR 
UOXOGHULBSOLIFBBWFLRVQQPRNGKSSO 
TWTQSJQSSEKZZWATJKLUDIAWINFBNYP 
VTTMZFPKWGDKZXTJCDIGKUHUAUEKCAR 
 
It is possible (I’d argue, likely) that these letters provide a clue applicable to the transposition 
phase of Part 4. They could be a key word in a columnar transposition or some indication of the 
way text is to be written in to, or taken out of, a grid. 
 
With these letters removed the letter count becomes 90.  Add back the leading ? that was once 
thought to belong to Part 3 and the count becomes 91 – a very attractive number, as it limits any 
full rectangle to being either 13 x 7 or 7 x 13.  (An alternative would be to look at the KR SO YP 
as a solid block to be removed.  Assume the T at the beginning of the 3rd line was a random 
occurrence and really does belong in the cipher text.  The ? becomes either a divider between 
sections or the question mark at the end of Can you see anything Q?  The count remains at 91.)

In either case, there are two pairs of Qs that are separated by exactly 13 letters.  Thus, if the text 
is written into a 13 x 7 grid, across the 13 columns and taken out by rows, those Qs end up 
paired. 
 
If, going back to an earlier assumption, Qs are used as nulls to hide the English frequency 
distribution, we might hope to find 4 or 5 Qs grouped together at the end of the text.  Getting them 
into pairs is at least a start. 
 
Now consider that the KR SO YP might indicate that letters are written in to, our taken out of, our 
grid two at a time, and an interesting possibility develops: 
 
A string of 5 Qs, taken 2 at a time would result in a lone Q and 2 pairs of Qs, which is exactly 
what shows up in the text. 
 
 
Testing Part 4 Speculations 
 
Is there any way to test the proposition that we have the correct substitution AND that a 13 x 7 
grid is a good starting point for the transposition. 
 
I propose that a suitable test would be to look at the unigram, bigram and trigram error statistics 
for the cipher text, substituted text prior to a 13 x 7 transposition, and substituted text after 
a 13 x 7 transposition. 
 
The cipher text should bare no relationship to real English.  Unigram error rates should be poor.  
Bigram and trigram stats should not change substantially when subjected to the 13 x 7 
transposition, as it would be random text in and random text out. 
 
The substituted text should show an improved Unigram stat, indicating text that more closely 
resembles English.  Since it is still in the incorrect order, bigram and trigram error rates should 
show little or no improvement. 
 
In the substituted text after a 13 x 7 transposition, the unigram stat would, be definition, 
remain unchanged.  However, we would predict that some of the letters are now paired up with 
their partners.  Thus bigram stats should improve.  If the write-in (or out) pattern involves 2 letters 
at a time (as with KR SO YP), we would expect to see no improvement in the trigram stats, as no 
trigram would have remained intact.  On the other hand, if KR SOT YP was used we should see 
some small improvement in the trigram because at least some of the letters were handled in 
groups of three.

Which text, the ? or the T is correct?  Although the results are too close to provide a definitive

SOT YP, which would have moved at least a small number of the letters as groups of three. 
 
 
A Final Speculation 
 
One last bit of off-the-wall speculation:  A 13 x 7 grid implies that we might still be looking for an 
additional clue, a 13-letter key to be used in a columnar transposition.   Elonka Dunin, after her 
dinner with Sanborn, remembered him saying that he put a great deal of effort into working on the 
right words for Part 1.  Part 1 is 13 words long, making BSSATAOFLTNOI (the first letter of each 
word) an attractive candidate for a 13-letter key. 
 
 
Summary 
 
At this point we have: 
 
1. Two candidates for the cipher text after substitution, with the ? version appearing 
somewhat more likely. 
2. A high probability that a 13 x 7 grid is involved in the transposition. 
3. Evidence that KR SOT YP provides some type of pattern for that transposition. 
4. Speculation that the words in Part 1 supply a 13-letter key word for the transposition.
