What makes Michael Hoerenberg's work (1) interesting is the tablar rows he 
chose.  The beginning of the alphabets ABCD, EFGH, around the ~ 30 character 
mark in the cipher -- they line up with what looks like "quiet" spots (less 
instances of higher numbered bases) if you graph the cipher in e.g. base 6.5. 
These quiet spots also seem to have base-5 rotational symmetry: NGKS, DIAW, 
INFB, originally brought up in Elonka's Kryptos group many years ago by Chris 
Feige in reference to Difference Arrays (2).  It's as though whatever 
obfuscation/encryption was applied, in these spots it doesn't have such a "noisy" 
effect.  
Hoerenberg's row selection from the Kryptos tablar and Chris Feige's symmetry 
aligned with cribs:

This geometry is post encryption, so it may not be useful as a deciphering tool.  
However, it might yield clues as to the underlying technique(s).  It's possible it's 
the result of an amalgamation/interplay of different shaped tablars. e.g. 
Something akin to Gromark tablars (whereby the length of the keyword defines 
the base) but possibly organized into different shapes on graph paper and/or a 
modular numbering system that constrains calculations to certain bases.  
Gromark, for example, uses a base-10 calculation.  See Richard Bean's work on 
Kryptos and Gromark (3).

[[page 4]]
A proposed solution formed solely from relationships/data within the cipher (no 
bible codes or assumptive thinking):
Segments/Cosets/Dimensions, not bases, to perform a symmetrical “change of 
basis”?  
•
Super simple, you just have to add. 
•
Can be worked visually, just swap “like” segments.  
•
It's always non-zero, always sums to [1..26], if first segments are static. 
•
Provides simultaneous substitution/transposition.  
•
Can be changed in a myriad of ways as long as there's symmetries and they
add to 26. 
•
Can do further modular math/substitution on the individual segments 
without breaking the system (e.g. rotation, index shuffling). 
•
“Segments” look a bit like Morse code.  :)
e.g. Key basis. Two fives, a ten, and a six = 26:
[x....], [.....], [..........], [......] = A
[.x...], [.....], [..........], [......] = B
…
[....x], [....x], [.........x], [....x.] = Y
[....x], [....x], [.........x], [.....x] = Z
e.g. Cipher basis.  Two eights, a four, and a six = 26:
[x.......], [........], [....], [......] = A
[.x......], [........], [....], [......] = B
…
[.......x], [.......x], [...x], [....x.] = Y
[.......x], [.......x], [...x], [.....x] = Z
etc.
I'm using the terms segment/coset/dimension interchangeably here, depending 
on the context in which you think of them.  Or, maybe equivalent fractions?
You're allowed to swap the “six” segments in the above basis example.  You could
also use clocks and compasses (e.g. 12s, 8s, & 6s) or geometric shapes to 
represent the segments.  You could swap among cipher rows, columns, etc. 
and/or literal spatial alignment of segments among rows/columns to define 
swapping rules among same-sized segments (e.g. four 5s and a 6). An Auto-key 
would work fine as well.
You can think of the x's in the example as a point in a multi-dimensional, finite 
topological space.  In the case of "A", it's just one point in one dimension.  
Conversely, "Z" is a point in the upper limit of all possible dimensions.  Then 
you're just swapping symmetrical dimensions, or groups of dimensions (not 
necessarily moving the points in those dimensions, though you can do that too).  

[[page 5]]
I'm not sure if this technically qualifies as a "change of basis," though the 
technique was inspired by it.
You could also group multiple segments and swap them, like Russian nested dolls,
as long as there's additive symmetry.  e.g. [two fives and a ten = 20] <-> [two 
eights and a four = 20].  This would effectively "shuffle" throughout the cipher 
many different segment sizes that could be further substituted/transposed.  This 
would "smooth out the bits" as Ed once said.  There might be further constraints 
at play to ensure an even distribution of "bits," preventing English language 
analysis (e.g. sticking to specific segment/coset/dimension sizes).
