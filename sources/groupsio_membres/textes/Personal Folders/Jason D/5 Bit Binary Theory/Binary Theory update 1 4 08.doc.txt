Since on the last DC meeting I have been trying to “Reinvent” the encryption technique used in K4 (since it is “unique”). I am doing this under the following assumptions

5 bit binary was used
The goal of the encryption system was to mask English
It can be done with pencil and Paper in a short period of time


First, I believe the “even out the 1’s and Zero’s” is an encryption clue not a decryption clue (although I was not there so who knows)

The reason for this is simple based on a few basic principles. We can assume that the goal of a well masked cipher text is that it resembles a randomly generated text, i.e. there will be no reoccurring patterns that can lead back to the encryption process. Therefore, if we understand what properties a randomly generated text will have, we can then try and invent a process that will take a plain text and quickly encrypt it (by hand) so it looks like random text.

To generate a random text, we can simply randomly generate letters.  Each letter will have a probability of being generated of 1/26 since the alphabet in question has 26 letters.  If we take a step back, however, and lets assume our alphabet has 32 letters. If we use 5 bits to encrypt this alphabet, each of the 32 binary numbers will correspond to one of the 32 letters.  Therefore if we want to generate a random alphabet of 32 letters, instead of randomly picking letters, we can randomly generate bits. As long as each bit has a 50/50 chance of being a 1 or 0, then the resultant text will be random.

The easiest way to convert a plaintext to a cipher text with an unequal # of 1’s and 0’s is to use an X/Or function where the key has an equal # of 1’s and 0’s.  As long as your key is long enough, or you properly scramble (transpose) the bits prior to or after the X/Or function, the resultant cipher text should be pretty random and very difficult to attack using frequency analysis.

This is what I think Ed means by evening out the 1’s and 0’s

However, we have two problems. We are only using 26 letters, not 32.  Therefore we have 6 numbers that cannot be formed as a result of the encryption process.  Also, since we have only 26 letters, we will not have a ratio of 50% 1’s and 50% 0’s in a randomly generated text. As it turns out a randomly generate text on average will have 43.85% 1’s and 56.15% 0’s. It is crucial we break this down further to the bit position level, Bits 1 and 2 (16X and 8X) will have a bit frequency of 38.46% 1’s, Bits 3 and 4 (4X and 2X) will have a bit frequency of 46.15%, and Bit 5 (1X) will have 50% 1’s.  Therefore, what every encryption process we develop should result in roughly these bit frequencies in the cipher text. Assuming it is sufficiently scrambled, it should appear like a random text.

So for our case, Even out the 1’s and 0’s does not mean 50/50 it means the proper proportion for a random text or 26 letters.

To create a method, we need to know what we are going from, i.e. English.  To find out the bit frequencies for English I pulled the Letter frequency chart from Wikipedia and multiplied them through each bit and then summed them.  For instance:  E occurs roughly 12.27 % of the time in English, we can multiply this through its binary representation 00100, to get the frequency of a 0 or 1 appearing in each spot.  When you sum them across all letters you get:
(each is the % of 1’s)
		    English   Random    K4
Bit 1 (16X)  29.76%    38.46%    41.2%
Bit 2  (8x)  32.56%	  38.46%    37.1%
Bit 3  (4x)  47.88%    46.15%    40.2%
Bit 4  (2X)  47.27%    46.15%    51.5%

Overall      40.13%    43.85%    43.3%

Bottom line:  We need to do some sort of substitution or other mathematical manipulation to increase the frequency of 1’s, and some sort of transposition to scramble the message enough so the original plaintext is not readily apparent. (Also note that K4 is only 2 1 bits away from mirroring the Random text from overall total)

Potential methods I have been working on:

Masking layer with selective substitution:
This is my favorite candidate thus far because: 

The substitution is minimal and can still be read as English,
It is very easy to approximate a random text.
It provides the mechanism for double letters at 6% chance (like K4) and provides an opportunity for a small amount of engineering the cipher text.
The only downside is it requires ~20% more cipher text than plaintext.

How it works:
From above English has a bit frequency around 40.13%. To create the cipher text we are simply going to divide the plain text into 4 parts. Each part goes through a partial substitution and then becomes a bit layer.

Part 1 40.13% -> Bit line 2  (8x) 38.46%   
Part 2 40.13% -> Bit line 3  (4x) 46.15%   
Part 3 40.13% -> Bit line 4  (2X) 46.15%
Part 4 40.13% - >Bit line 5  (1X)  50%

To achieve the necessary increase in 1’s for bit lines 3, 4 and 5, we are going to partially substitute letters 26 through 32 for A, B C,D E F respectively:

For examples
A= 00000  with a=11010  3 additional 1 bits
B= 00001  with b=11011  3 additional 1 bits
C= 00010  with c=11100  2 additional 1 bits
D= 00011  with d=11101  2 additional 1 bits
E= 00100  with e=11110  3 additional 1 bits
F= 00101  with f=11111  3 additional 1 bits

For parts 2 and 3 we only need to substitute 2-3 letters (6 1 bits) per 20 letters to get to the correct frequency, For part 4 we only need 3-4 letters (9-10 1 bits) per 20 letters to get to the correct frequency.

For Part 1: No substitution as we are only 1.5 bits per hundred off.  For a very long message we could periodically swap the masking line (16X bits) with the 8X line to get the desired frequency.
