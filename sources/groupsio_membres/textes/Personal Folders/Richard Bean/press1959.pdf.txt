INTRODUCTION 
The field of dynamics is largely con­
cerned with the response of systems (me­
chanical, electrical, etc.) to various types 
of disturbances. 
A number of techniques 
exist for the analysis of certain types of 
disturbances and these have found wide ap­
plication in many fields. 
The use of res­
ponses to step inputs, pulse inputs, and 
sinusoidal inputs is already familiar and 
useful in many aeronautical areas. 
Fourier methods in particular have found 
wide regions of application in aeronautics 
and have provided many useful results. In 
these cases, the methods of analysis permit 
the synthesis of responses for linear sys­
tems to certain types of arbitrary but speci­
fied disturbances. 
However, if the disturb­
ances are aperiodic and nondissipati ve, these 
methods will generally no longer apply. 
Furthermore, if the disturbances contain an 
element of chance or randomness and cannot 
be defined precisely, but only by certain 
probability distributions and average values, 
the foregoing methods can no longer be used 
and more general techniques are required. 
The introduction of a chance or random 
element leads naturally to the application of 
probability and statistical notions which in 
combination with classical Fourier tech­
niques have in recent years evolved into a 
general technique for the analysis of prob­
lems of this type. These techniques stem 
from the field of random process theory 
and from generalized harmonic analysis. 
Of particular appropriateness for present 
purposes are the methods centered around 
the use of power spectra. 
The motivation and early development 
of power spectral techniques had its roots 
in studies of economic time series at the 
turn of the century. In the physical sciences, 
the first applications were concerned with 
the problems of light, the Brownian motion 
of particles, and turbulence. 
The develop­
ment of a rigorous mathematical theory is 
?ven more recent and appears to be largely 
due to Kolmogoroff and Wiener. 
Prior to 
and during the last war, many useful ap­
plications of these techniques were made in 
the fields of communications and servo-
mechanisms. 
The applications of these techniques to 
aeronautical problems appear to have been 
initiated since the last war and are thus 
still in an early stage of development. 
Already, the list of aeronautical problems 
to which these techniques have been use­
fully applied include: 
(a) Gust loads on airplanes 
(b) Buffeting loads 
(c) Acoustic noises 
(d) Tracking errors and human response 
studies 
(e) Wind tunnel turbulence 
(f) 
Runway roughness (land and sea) 
(g) Electronic noises in guidance and 
gunnery systems. 
Refs. 1 to 24 provide a partial list of un­
classified papers in this area. Although this 
list is already impressive, there will no 
doubt be a further rapid development in these 
areas and extensions to new areas of ap­
plication. 
In spite of the large amount of activity 
in these areas of aeronautics, there still 
appears to be some lack of appreciation 
of the basic motivation of power spectral 
techniques, the fundamental relations and 
their limitations, and some of the problems 
encountered in applications. This condition 
has in part arisen from the specialized 
nature of the applications so far made, the 
scattered nature of the literature, and the 
lack of definitive treatments in the engi­
neering literature. 
The application of these techniques in 
flight test studies also places some special 
IVC:1 

[[page 4]]
requirements on instrument characteristics, 
flight test techniques, and data reduction 
procedures. For these reasons, it appeared 
appropriate to include a paper in this volume 
which would at least survey the basic notions, 
the fundamental relations of these newer 
techniques and their relation to classical 
techniques, and some of the problems en­
countered in applications. 
If the disturbance is characterized by 
certain probability distributions or average 
values, then it is necessary to consider 
all disturbances having these characteristics. 
Another way to say this is that we are con­
cerned not with a single time function, but 
an ensemble of time functions having the 
required properties. 
We shall require an 
analytic representation for such ensembles 
of functions and rules for performing input-
output or response calculations. 
Also, in practice, finite and incomplete 
data are the rule; a small piece of one time 
history is frequently available and must be 
used to estimate results for more general 
situations. 
This is the familiar inference 
problem of the statistician. 
This matter, 
in the present case, turns out to be far from 
a trivial one and the problems that stem from 
it can be largely blamed for apparent incon­
sistencies in applications, and for the long 
delay in the practical applications of these 
general techniques. In fact, a clear apprécia -
tion of these problems and useful results 
in this respect are only very recent achieve­
ments (Ref. 25) and will be considered herein. 
The order of presentation will start with a 
brief discussion of the classical techniques 
for response determination to arbitrary dis­
turbances. The concepts of power spectra and 
the relations between the spectra and time-
history characteristics will then be briefly 
reviewed and the relations between the power 
spectra of a disturbance and the response to 
the disturbance for a linear system will then 
be presented. The practical problem of meas­
uring power spectra from finite data will 
then be considered and digital and analog 
techniques discussed. Finally in the interest 
of concreteness, some examples of results 
obtained in aeronautical applications will be 
described.

So far, we have considered arbitrary but 
determinate (nonrandom) functions and have 
been able for certain cases to obtain their

representation by superposition of elemental 
type functions such as sines and cosines, 
unit steps, and impulses. There exist many 
disturbances in the physical, engineering, and 
social sciences as well as in aeronautics 
where the disturbance of interest is not so 
well defined. 
Fig. IVC:1 illustrates three 
examples of this kind that occur in aero­
nautics. 
This indefiniteness arises from an ele­
ment of chance or randomness which affects 
the details of the function. A simple example 
is furnished by the fluctuations of buffeting 
pressure as measured at a point on a wing. 
If this test were repeated, the exact time 
history would not be repeated exactly, but 
the essential character, averages, or other 
statistical characteristics could be expected 
to be unchanged. 
If we are interested in the buffeting fluc­
tuations, then our method of representation 
may not be very adequate if we describe only 
one test result. We would instead prefer to 
use a representation which adequately des­
cribes the ensemble of functions that would 
be obtained if the test were repeated many, 
many times. 
This ensemble of functions is 
referred to as the random process. In many 
physical cases, this ensemble can be repre­
sented in a simple and useful manner by means 
of random process theory. Accounts of the 
mathematical theory of random processes 
may be found in Refs. 26 to 29. 
In the present paper, we shall restrict 
ourselves to stationary processes; that is, 
functions whose characteristics do not change 
with time. This restriction is not too serious 
although useful results have already been ob­
tained 
for 
nonstationary or evolutionary 
processes. However, the greatest simplifica­
tion and usefulness is associated with the 
stationary processes. 
Some of the results to follow are restricted 
to Gaussian processes.

(g) Significance of Power Spectral 
Techniques 
The foregoing discussion has served to 
indicate the basic concepts and relations of 
the power spectral and random process 
techniques. 
These results have some far-
reaching implications in regard to aero­
nautical problems involving random dis­
turbances. They provide a systematic math­
ematical framework and technique for the 
analysis of such problems which formerly 
were treated by empirical and intuitive 
methods which were frequently arbitrary. 
Some of the more important implications 
that should be noted are the following: 
(1) The power spectrum provides a 
compact mathematical representation of a 
random process. 
(2) Relative to random disturbances, 
the significant characteristics of a linear 
dynamic system are contained in the fre­
quency response function. 
(3) The significant characteristics 
of the response of linear systems to random 
disturbances may be calculated simply (al­
though the calculated information is only 
complete in the case of a Gaussian dis­
turbance). 
(4) A need exists for detailed in­
formation on the spectral characteristics 
of the random processes that occur in 
aeronautical applications. 
(5) These analytical techniques pro­
vide a means of obtaining results that can 
be directly correlated with test data for 
random type disturbances. 
(6) Response to multiple disturb­
ances, taking into account 
interrelations 
between the disturbances, may be obtained 
in a systematic fashion. 
(7) Perhaps 
the 
most 
important 
result offered by these techniques is the 
new insight into the dynamic interrelations 
between disturbances and system responses. 
This new insight provides a valuable guide 
in planning new theoretical and experimental 
investigations. 
(8) Information on the random type 
input may be obtained readily from a knowl­
edge of the system frequency response char­
acteristics and measurements of the system 
response. 
(9) The significance of the effects 
of changes in the frequency response function 
on the response to a random disturbance can 
be evaluated in a systematic fashion.

Prewhitening 
Because the errors and distortion in­

constant with frequency, the least pressing 
demands on accuracy will be made by mod­

about the same size at all frequencies. 
Demands on accuracy and, conversely, 
degradation of results from computational 
noise and distortion may often be reduced 
by analyzing the spectrum of a transformed 
noise, whose spectrum has been altered 
to a known extent and in such a direction 
as to make its spectrum more nearly white. 
Such a transformation will be called pre­
whitening. 
(It is, of course, understood 
that when prewhitening is used, the resulting

also

(a) Disturbance Characteristics 
The relatively complete theory existing 
for the case of a Gaussian disturbance, as 
has been indicated, has made this type of 
random process of particular interest. Thus, 
in many studies, efforts have been made to 
determine the degree to which the actual 
random processes encountered in aeronau­
tical problems can be represented by a 
Gaussian process. 
In almost all cases, 
testing of observed measurements has been 
restricted to the consideration of the overall 
probability distribution. 
It will be recalled that a Gaussian process 
is characterized by much more stringent 
conditions; namely, that the multivariate 
distribution be Gaussian. 
However, the in­
dications of the overall probability distri­
bution, while inconclusive, are a very helpful 
guide in most cases. 
Fortunately, in most 
of the applications so far made, the processes 
observed appear to be Gaussian to a good 
degree of approximation. Some of the quan­
tities which appear to be so characterized 
at least for some conditions are atmospheric 
turbulence, airplane loads in rough air, buf­
feting loads, ocean wave heights, and pres­
sure fluctuations in acoustic noise. 
Some 
experimental data in this regard are given 
in Refs. 5, 8, and 9. 
Spectra of Atmospheric Turbulence. As 
an example of the results obtained in ex­
perimental measurements of power spectra, 
some measurements of the spectrum of 
atmospheric turbulence will be described. 
A number of flight measurements of tur­
bulence spectra have recently been made 
using airplanes, and some of these results 
obtained from various sources are sum­
marized in Fig. IVC:6. 
The ordinate of Fig. IVC:6 is the power 
spectral density, the abscissa is frequency 
in radians per foot. The frequency argument 
Ω = 2 irI λ where λ is the gust wavelength, 
is useful for airplane studies since insofar 
as the airplane is concerned, turbulence 
is essentially a spatial phenomenon since 
the movement of "eddies" is slow compared 
to the motion of the airplane. 
The data shown, in most cases, are for 
the vertical component of the turbulence. 
In one case, marked by the squares, the 
data are for the horizontal component or 
longitudinal turbulence. 
The data cover 
a range of gust wavelengths of from 10 to 
3000 feet, which is the region that is of most 
concern for many airplane problems. 
The spectra differ in intensity as indi­
cated by the height of the spectra reflecting 
the variation of turbulence intensity with 
the weather conditions. 
The spectra, in 
each case, show rapid decreases in power 
with increasing frequency. 
In fact, the 
spectra vary roughly as 1/ii , a result in 
general agreement with theoretical results 
obtained in the theory of isotropie turbulence. 
The general shape of these spectra, with 
rapid decreases in power at the higher fre­
quencies, leads to some important impli­
cations in regard to the design of airplanes 
for smoother flight. 
Because of the general characteristics 
of these spectra, it has been convenient in 
theoretical studies to use the analytical 
expression for the turbulence spectrum shown 
on Fig. IVC:7. 
This expression has been 
useful in wind tunnel studies of turbulence 
and can be seen to have the general char­
acteristics of the measured spectra of at­
mospheric turbulence.

The equation has two parameters, the 
mean square gust velocity Ü2", which des­
cribes the turbulence intensity, and the 
so-called scale of turbulence L. The scale 
of turbulence L can, in a rough sense, be 
considered to be proportional to the average 
eddy size. 
The curves in Fig. IVC:7 are 
for a mean square gust velocity equal to 
1 and for values of L = 200, 600, 1000, and 
1500 feet. The curves at higher frequencies 
all approach a shape of 1/il2, but differ 
in the frequency at which the flattening out 
occurs. 
For large values of L, most of the 
power is at the very low frequencies. 
Comparison of these curves with the 
measurements of Fig. IVC:6 and other meas­
urements of the spectrum of atmospheric 
turbulence have suggested that a representa­
tive value of L for atmospheric turbulence 
is of the order of a thousand feet. 
For 
studies of wind tunnel turbulence, L may 
be expected to be much smaller, perhaps 
on the order of an inch. 
For design purposes, the overall gust 
experience in operations is of concern. 
Presumably, the overall experience consists 
of various exposure times to each of the 
spectra of Fig. IVC:6 as well as for other 
spectra of turbulence associated with dif­
ferent weather conditions. 
The available 
spectra measurements have suggested that, 
as a first approximation, the spectral shape 
might be considered fixed and only the in­
tensity or root-mean-square gust velocity 
considered to vary. 
Thus, the overall gust 
experience may be considered to consist of 
exposure to turbulence of variable root-
mean-square gust velocity, but invariant 
spectral shape. 
On this basis, operational data on gust 
loads have been used in Refs. 18 and 19 
to estimate the probability distribution of 
the 
root-mean-square 
gust 
velocity for 
various types of airplane operations and for 
various weather conditions. 
These results 
provide a basis for the calculation of the 
airplane gust response experience in op­
erations. 
Spectra measurements have also been 
made in regard to other aeronautical prob­
lems. 
Fig. IVC:8 taken from Ref. 16 shows 
some results that have been obtained in regard 
to runway roughness. The figure shows the 
spectra that have been obtained for sections 
of two runways at Langley Air Force Base, 
Virginia. 
One of these runways was con­
sidered, as designated on the figure, a re­
latively smooth runway while the other runway 
was considered a relatively rough runway 
and was, in fact, no longer in use. Further 
measurements of the runway spectra are 
currently underway. 
Fig. IVC:9 shows the results of some 
measurements obtained on the noise from 
a jet engine (reported in Ref. 21). The 
figure shows the power spectra of pressure 
fluctuation as measured at two positions 
in the sound field, as indicated by the sketch 
on the upper right hand side of the figure. 
A large number of such measurements have 
been made in order to define the noise field 
about jet engines and about propeller-driven 
aircraft. 
These measurements of disturbance spec­
tra have provided at least an initial basis 
for the calculation of various airplane re­
sponses; for example, the turbulence spectra 
have been useful in studies of airplane gust 
loadings, runway spectra have been useful 
for studies of airplane taxying loads and 
the noise spectra have been useful in the 
study of the noise nuisance and in the study 
of fatigue failures of panels due to noise. 
(b) The Effect of Low Damping in Short 
Period on Loads in Rough Air 
One of the first analytical results ob­
tained in the application of power spectral 
techniques to the gust load problem was 
the indication of the importance of the short 
period damping on the loads in rough air, 
Ref. 5. 
Experimental verification of this 
result was obtained in a test in which a 
rocket-propelled model was flown through

(Ref. 8). 
The model covered a range of 
Mach numbers from about 0.60 to 1.00 which 
also included a fairly large range of longi­
tudinal stability conditions. The period and 
cycles to damp to one-tenth amplitude of 
the 
short period oscillation for the test 
model is shown as a function of Mach number 
in Fig. 1VC:10 and indicates a fairly rapid 
deterioration of the short period damping 
above a Mach number of about 0.9. 
The test model was a tailless missile 
and was fired through rough air at the NACA 
test center at Wallops Island, 
Virginia. 
The total missile flight lasted only about 
20 seconds and yielded 2 seconds of usable 
record at each of eight test speeds. Inasmuch

necessarily small, there was some doubt 
that statistically significant test results could 
be obtained with this technique. Fig. IVC:11 
shows sample time histories of the missile

airspeeds of roughly 750 miles per hour and 
600 miles per hour.

and this can be seen qualitatively to be the 
case from the relative magnitude of the 
fluctuations. The missile vertical accelera­
tion in both cases is seen to be somewhat 
irregular although the predominating motion 
is seen to occur at a frequency of roughly 
10 cycles per second. The frequency char­
acteristics of the records will be considered 
in more detail in connection with the power 
spectra. 
The frequency distributions of the ac­
celeration amplitude were determined from 
the records (readings at 0.01 second) and 
are shown in Fig. IVC:12 for several test 
speeds or Mach numbers. The dashed lines 
are fitted normal distributions. The reason­
able fit of the normal distribution curves 
suggests that the process may be considered 
to have a Gaussian amplitude distribution.

seen from the relative areas or the root-
mean-square values shown, suggest rather 
rapid increases in loads for the speed range 
covered.
