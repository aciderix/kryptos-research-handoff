Preface
In March, 2013, I discovered that K4 uses multiple steps (as many had suspected), 
and decoded one of the steps.  The first step uses cryptography that I am sure 
would not surprise those that solved K1 through K3.
On May 20, 2013, 2 1/2 years after Jim Sanborn released the BERLIN clue, I 
announced this to the Kryptos Yahoo Group.
The 97 characters in the next step of K4 are (split into two lines for 
readability):
PGZFGTEZPFCYGFLJLZGSOJAILWDCCNZFHSHQHIVZKUCGOZAAF
JEVHOCZVSNHYEHBERLINPEEKRRRHBUXJOPYFFTXBMMQMOFQS
I do not have a background in cryptography, so I am just a novice (who, through 
some combination of luck or skill, figured out part of K4).
Following are some thoughts on how to proceed from here.

Thought #1:  A Mask
One of the most noticeable features of the partial decoding of K4 is the presence 
of BERLIN, which is generally assumed to be in the final decoding.
This leads to two possibilities:  [1] The final text does not include BERLIN (with 
the word Berlin having randomly appeared in the middle of the decoding steps), or 
[2] Berlin does appear in the final decoding.
I find #1 unlikely.  Having looked at perhaps thousands of incorrect decodings of 
K4, many have short English words in them.  However, getting a 6 letter word is 
fairly rare – especially one that has such a clear relation to Kryptos (e.g. with 
the Berlin wall monument at the CIA headquarters near Kryptos).  And many people 
would call “foul” if the final text of K4 did not indeed have BERLIN where the clue 
suggested it would be.
So if we assume that BERLIN is in the final decoded text where it appears in this 
partial solution, I think that there must be a mask of some sort involved 
(something that allows further decoding of some characters, but not others, 
essentially allowing the BERLIN letters to bypass further decoding).
The most obvious mask I could see was the morse code in Kryptos.  My thought was to 
put the morse code together in one string, and where the extra letters 'e' appeared 
no encoding would be done.
As an example, let's say the the ciphertext is “BERLINPEEKRRRHB”, mask is 
“eeeeeeINVISIBLE” (with any 'e' outside a word meaning 'do not decode', anything 
else meaning 'encode'), and the encoding is a simple Caesar cipher (add 1 to the 
letter, so 'A' becomes 'B').  In this case, it would decode to BERLINQFFLSSSIC.  
The mask allows further decoding while still letting BERLIN through.
We would have:
ciphertext: ...BERLINPEEKRRRHB...
mask:
...eeeeeeINVISIBLE...
plaintext:
...BERLINQFFLSSSIC...
Of course, this is simplistic (e.g. the 'RRR' likely requires multiple alphabets), 
but shows how a mask might work.
I did try as many possible variations of the morse code as I could (as it it not 
clear exactly the order that morse code snippets would go in), without luck.
If a mask is used, and it lets BERLIN through, the mask would need to be at least 
97 characters long (or repeated to that length).  Also, it would need to have at 
least 6 “do not decode this letter” marks in it in a row (like the 'e's in the 
morse code) to allow BERLIN through.

Thought #2:  A 14-Character Key
Running some statistics on the partial solution to K4 (specifically, the periodic 
index of coincidence ) reveals that there may be a 14-character key involved.  I

Here, you can see that the index of coincidence is highest for a key length of 14.  
Unfortunately, I am not experienced enough in ciphers to know how likely a key 
length of 14 is given this data (especially given the short length of ciphertext 
and the possibility of a mask).  If a periodic cipher is used with a key (such as 
Vigenere), however, the data suggests a 14-character key.
Further, the duplicated letters in the partial solution of K4 are “ZF” (at 
positions 2 and 30), and “HB” (at positions 62 and 76).  The difference between the 
“ZF” positions is 28 (a multiple of 14), and the difference between the “HB” 
positions is 14.  I believe this helps increase the chances that a 14-character 
period is used.
So where could one find a 14-character key?  Using a word that long is very 
unlikely; my guess would be it has to do with there being 14 rows on the panels.
Breaking down the partial solution into 14-character segments results in:

CGOZAAFJEVHOCZ
VSNHYEHBERLINP
EEKRRRHBUXJOPY
FFTXBMMQMOFQS 
The idea of a 14-character key comes from the PLCCVEF (first column) using one 
alphabet, GJNGSEF (second column) using another, and so forth.  That could be a 
Vigenere cipher, or something completely different.
However, one would still need to account for BERLIN staying where it is (assuming, 
of course, it does stay there).  The repeated HB suggests that the 'H' before 
BERLIN is indeed plaintext, and that the second HB is plaintext as well.
I tried some common methods of attacking the partial decoding with 14-letter keys, 
but none worked for me.  That could be due to some letters not being decoded (e.g. 
BERLIN staying as-is with a mask), or it could be due to multiple steps being 
required.

Thought 3:  Two Halves; K1+K2 Alignment
Something that is quite apparent (to me, at least) is that the first half of K4 
seems somehow different than the second half of K4.  It's hard to place in words 
how it seems different.  And it may be more than just the halves.  For example, 
splitting the partial K4 solution into quarters shows an IC (Index of Coincidence) 
of the 1st and 3rd quarters being identical, with the 2nd and 4th quarters lower.
This may just be due to the short length of K4.  Statistics work better and better 
on longer and longer lengths of ciphertext.
The section after this has some of the statistics.
Next, with the idea of Kryptos perhaps being a palimpsest, I noted that K1 is 63 
bytes, with K2 starting at the 64th character of the first panel.  Interestingly, 
BERLIN starts at the 64th position of K4.
Encoded K1 (bold) and start of K2:
EMUFPHZLRFAXYUSDJKZLDKRNSHGNFIVJ
YQTQUXQBQVYUVLLTREVJYQTMKYRDMFD
VFPJUDEEHZWETZYVGWHKKQETGFQJNCE
GGW
Partially decoded K4 (BERLIN in bold):
PGZFGTEZPFCYGFLJLZGSOJAILWDCCNZF
HSHQHIVZKUCGOZAAFJEVHOCZVSNHYEH
BERLINPEEKRRRHBUXJOPYFFTXBMMQMO
FQS
This gives the possibility that the first half of K4 is somehow related to K1, with 
the second half of K4 is somehow related to K2.
My hunch is that K4 is indeed split into two (or more) sections somehow, but that 
the positioning of BERLIN starting at the 64th position (correlating to the start 
of K2) is just a coincidence.

Thought 5:  Length
There are 97 letters in K4.  That poses a problem for some ciphers, as 97 is a 
prime number (no two numbers multiplied together result in 97).
If a cipher is involved that requires the use of a non-prime number of characters, 
I would find 98 characters to be the most likely (perhaps including the '?' at the 
end of K3, or perhaps a null character).  98 would work into 7 14-character lines, 
which fits in nicely with the probability of a 14-character key.  It also fits in 
nicely with the solution to K3, where Jim Sanborn's chart has 7 lines.
