ABSTRACT 
A hypothesized on approaching the decryption of K4 from Jim 
Sanborn’s Kryptos (1990) sculpture located within the 
grounds of the CIA Headquarters. Existing information on 
Kryptos was combined with historical research on 
cryptography methods, the artist Jim Sanborn, and former 
CIA communications officer Ed Scheidt to develop an 
inference model of K4 and hypothesize an approach. Based 
on our findings, it may be very likely K4 has been encrypted 
through a multi‐step process involving one‐time pad and 
several masks. 
INTRODUCTION 
On July 12th, 2019, we stumbled upon a YouTube video 
published by Great Big Story titled Cracking the 
Uncrackable Code and a thumbnail of repeating KRYPTOS 
characters.  Immediately this caught our eyes and due to 
our unique skills and interests in computers/mathematics, 
and espionage history/art, we immediately decided to 
tackled his unsolved puzzle. 
In our initial research of the puzzle, we learned that Jim 
Sanborn’s Kryptos is a set of sculptures commissioned by 
the CIA Fine Arts Commission to coincide with the opening 
of the New Headquarters Building (NHB) in 1991. 
Comprised of multiple rock formations and sculptures, its 
centerpiece is a curved 4 panel copperplate screen with 
1,735 characters cut out. On the right side is an encoded 
text and the left a series of characters arranged in the style 
of a 16th century Vigenere Tableau. In the encoded text, 
there are presumably 4 sections that have been designated 
K1 through K4 and Morse code messages scattered within 
the rock sculpture formations collectively known as K0. As

Historical Era 
The importance of a historical era in cryptography is that 
the method of encryption used should represent the 
appropriateness with respect to time. An example is that 
WPA2 WiFi encryption would not be used in the 1800’s, but 
invisible ink would be appropriate for the 1800’s.

One‐time Pad 
The mechanics of one-time pad is an evolutionary step in 
substitution ciphers that can shift each individual character 
to any of the possible 26 A-Z letters. What differs from the 
prior substitution methods is the special use of keys. 
Firstly, the single use would make brute-force decryption 
of multiple messages difficult. Secondly while the key can 
be of any length, it would be most strongly encrypted if the 
key is at least the length of the plaintext and of random 
(unpredictable) text.  
In order to utilize one-time pad, there are separate 
encryption and decryption methods. First, each English 
alphabet letter is assigned a number. A is assigned 0, B is 
assigned 1, C is assigned 3 all the way to Z being assigned 
25. 
For the encryption step, the process would be: 
1. Plaintext character converted to number 
2. ADD number of shifts from key (can be letter 
equivalent or number) 
3. Divide by mod 26 
4. Convert new character into equivalent letter 
(ciphertext) 
The mod 26 will account for the shift when the letter goes past Z 
and will return to A, B, C, etc. 
The decryption method would be the opposite: 
1. Convert ciphertext into number 
2. SUBTRACT number of shifts from key (can be letter 
equivalent or number) 
3. Divide by mod 26 
4. Revert back to plaintext character

“BERLIN” & “CLOCK” Clues 
Mr. Sanborn has been extremely generous to provide 
several clues in 2010 and 2014 regarding K4. He indicated 
to the New York Times that positions 64 to 69 and positions 
70 to 74 spell BERLINCLOCK when decoded from NYPVTT 
and MZFPK. Using a one-time pad, the key for positions 64 
to 74 would be MUYKLGKORNA as a decode key. For 
example, the N (value 13) would need 12 shifts backwards 
(i.e. letter M) to reach the letter B. 
While critics may discount the one-time pad method (due 
to the absence of a key and the ability to create any 
plaintext), what is crucial is the letter frequency of K4 in 
terms of M and Y – there is one a single M and Y on the 97 
K4 cipher text. Currently the M and Y on the cipher text are 
on the 70th and 65th positions respectively and somehow 
would have to shift to get into the 64th and 66th position 
respectively. If this is the case, K4 would act both as the 
ciphertext and public key in a one-time pad decryption. To 
increase the level of difficulty, the K4 public key would 
likely have to be rearranged.

cipher methods as deterrents to computational methods.

linear human approaches. Assuming a public key on K4 is 
used for the one-time pad cipher, there should be at least 
two masks done.  
One mask for the public key in terms of multiple 
transpositions (matching the redundant technique in K1 
and K2 substitutions and the double transposition of K3).
