Analyzing the Kryptos tableau panels to determine cipher 
In a standard Vigenère tableau (tabula recta), the letters begin A-Z for the first encryption/decryption line, and is 
shifted by 25 characters forward (1 character back) for each subsequent line. The plaintext is then encoded by a 
repeating keyword that serves as the lookup table. The decode is simply utilizing the same key in the opposite 
direction to obtain the plaintext. For the Kryptos tableau panels, it begins with the A-Z order on the first line, but the 
second line the letters KRYPTOSABCDE are used. This modification to the alphabetic order is utilized for Quagmire 
family ciphers. 
Quagmire Ciphers and KRYPOS in tableau panels 
Unlike the standard Vigenère cipher, the Quagmire family uses dual key system of a ciphertext key (KRYPTOS - 
horizontal) and an indicator key (i.e. PALIMPSEST - vertical).

Determining key length from repeating segments 
What is important to note for the Quagmire and Vigenère ciphers is that there is a repetition of the key throughout 
the ciphertext. That is, eventually certain groups such as THE or OF will begin to repeat itself as the ciphertext length 
increases. By measuring the distance between the repeating segments, one can determine a possible key length. With 
the observation VJYQT repeating from position 31-35 and 51-55, there appears to be a period of 5, 10 or 20 to the 
key. This means the key itself is likely a word of 5, 10, or 20 characters in length1.   
Stenographic clues 
While it is possible to try all three key possibilities, a stenographic clue in the tableau greatly aids in the process to 
determine a key length of 10 characters. By examining the tableau in greater detail, the P on the 2nd line and G on the 
12th line of the tableau is sticking out, giving hint to the size of the tableau (11 characters from PTOSABCDEFG on the 
right side). What is most critical about stenographic clues is that the information from the artist must be retained in 
all iterations of CIA Kryptos and mini-Kryptos versions (nickel-silver & copper editions). The WIRED 2005 version 
also may also originate from the artist as the blueprint for mini-Kryptos (see above). The original CIA Kryptos has 
the P & G of the tableau sticking out on the right, and the small copper Kryptos in Jim Sanborn’s studio during the 
Great Big Story (2019) video has the P & G leftwards. Regardless of the positioning, the P & G provide vital

the cipher line position relative to other letters. In the case of K1, the Quagmire III cipher has been identified so that

Conclusion 
K1 can most definitely be solved through a pencil and paper method, but will require grid paper, scissors (for Caesar 
shifts of each indicator line) and a dictionary to look up the word palimpsest. Codebreakers are extremely fortunate 
that Jim Sanborn used the English language for the Quagmire III keys and without it, likely the brute-force autokey 
method would be necessary. Some of the weaknesses exploited on the Quagmire III solution were: 
1. Cyclical nature of encryption (repeated use of the key) to results in repeated segments – the longer the 
ciphertext the most likely the words OF and THE will appear 
2. Symmetrical nature of polyalphabetic substitution – for each indicator line, the ciphertext and plaintext is 
both decoded and encoded by the same key 
3. Relationships of alphabet characters to each other (shifts between characters are preserved in plaintext and 
ciphertext alphabet ordering) 
4. English words used as keys to the cipher – the advent of computing can brute force such ciphers 
The bottom half of the tableau might also be useful for the decryption of K2 as the top half was for K1. 
K4 Prediction 
K4 is likely a multi step encryption that may use a substitution cipher of some sorts. To increase the difficulty, the 
weaknesses identified above can be mitigated by: 
1. Non-repeating key at least to the length of the plaintext 
2. An asymmetrical encryption method where the encoding key is different from the decoding key 
3. Independent positional character shifts (not in relation to any other text positions) 
4. Non-English key akin to gibberish AND 
5. Extra concealment or masking of the plaintext as phonetically spelled words (four as P-H-O-A-R) 
All of these weaknesses can be mitigated with a one-time pad, XOR, or Vernam cipher. To increase the difficulty, 
there would likely be an unconventional K4 transposition required prior to the decode. The best guess is that K4 is 
literally a public key OTP decode key that must be manipulated through transposition (and or substitution) to 
decrypt K4. This would be akin to a combination lock or a word lock where the key is merely scrambled. 
The BERLIN and CLOCK clue from 2010 and 2014 respectively provides hints that if a one-time pad decode for 
NYPVTT and MZFPK is required for positions 64-69 and 70-74 respectively, the decode key would simply be 
MUYKLGKORNA. Coincidentally there is only a single M and Y in K4. If the K1 intuitive method shown above was any 
indication to the approach of K4, it is that some form of shifting or rotating might be incorporated on the decoding 
to significantly reduce the need for complex mathematics. The most elegant transpositional method would be 
utilizing a Jefferson disk or M-94 military cipher to manipulate the K4 public key prior to OTP decoding.
